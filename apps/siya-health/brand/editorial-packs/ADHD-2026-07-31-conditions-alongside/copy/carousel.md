# Carousel — 5 Conditions That Commonly Occur Alongside ADHD

**Insight:** ADHD rarely exists in isolation. Symptoms overlap, conditions coexist, one
condition can mask another — and treating only one may not fully address someone's
difficulties.

**Practical change:** If symptoms don't fit neatly into one diagnosis, pursue a
comprehensive evaluation that covers attention, mood, anxiety, trauma, sleep, and eating
patterns — do not self-diagnose from social media.

**Tone locks:** calm · reassuring · physician-led · no fear language · ADHD is never
presented as explaining every symptom · "overlap / coexist / assessment / whole person"
reinforced on every slide.

## Slide 1 — Hook

**Headline:** 5 Conditions That Often Occur With ADHD
**Support:** Treating one while missing another can delay recovery.

---

## Slide 2 — Depression (1 of 5)

**Recognition:** Low mood, low drive — and focus that never comes back.
**Explanation:** ADHD and depression often occur together. Both can drain motivation and
concentration. [1][2]
**Takeaway:** Low motivation isn't always "just depression."

Caption-layer evidence: reported prevalence of depression in adults with ADHD varies
widely across studies — roughly 19–53% [2] — so we state "often occur together" on-frame
and keep numbers in the caption/reference layer only as ranges.

---

## Slide 3 — Anxiety disorders (2 of 5)

**Recognition:** Constant worry makes focusing even harder.
**Explanation:** Roughly half of adults with ADHD also meet criteria for an anxiety
disorder — and each can feed the other. [1][2]
**Takeaway:** Trouble focusing can come from anxiety, ADHD, or both.

Evidence: NCS-R found ~47% of adults with ADHD had a comorbid anxiety disorder [1];
reviews report up to ~50% [2]. ADHD-related disorganization and missed deadlines can
generate anxiety; worry independently degrades concentration.

---

## Slide 4 — Bipolar disorder (3 of 5)

**Recognition:** Impulsivity and restlessness show up in both.
**Explanation:** Bipolar disorder brings distinct mood episodes. ADHD traits stay steady
over time. Treatments differ significantly. [3][4]
**Takeaway:** Getting the diagnosis right matters.

Evidence: meta-analysis of 71 studies — ~1 in 13 adults with ADHD also has bipolar
disorder; ~1 in 6 adults with bipolar disorder has ADHD [3]. The clinical differentiator
is episodicity: bipolar symptoms deviate from the person's baseline in episodes; ADHD is
chronic and trait-like [4]. Wording is deliberately careful — no on-frame prevalence
number, no implication that the reader can self-differentiate.

---

## Slide 5 — PTSD (4 of 5)

**Recognition:** Trauma can affect attention, memory, and emotional balance.
**Explanation:** PTSD and ADHD can look alike — and some people live with both. Trauma
history belongs in every thorough assessment. [5][6]
**Takeaway:** Trauma and ADHD can overlap — but they are not the same.

Evidence: meta-analysis shows a bidirectional association (relative risk ~2.9 for PTSD in
ADHD vs healthy controls) [5]; adult comorbidity estimates vary widely across studies
(~28–36% in one qualitative synthesis) and are not stated on-frame to avoid false
precision [6].

---

## Slide 6 — Binge eating disorder (5 of 5)

**Recognition:** Impulsivity can reach eating patterns too.
**Explanation:** Binge eating disorder is several times more common in people with ADHD.
Both conditions are treatable. [7]
**Takeaway:** Eating patterns may be part of a larger clinical picture.

Evidence: meta-analysis — pooled odds ratio ~4 for binge eating disorder in ADHD;
prevalence of BED in ADHD samples ~9–11% [7]. Some of the association may be accounted
for by co-occurring depression/anxiety [8] — hence "can contribute," never "causes."
