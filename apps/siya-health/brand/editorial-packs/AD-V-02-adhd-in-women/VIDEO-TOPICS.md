### Script — Hook A (dialogue)

**Partner / interviewer:** Why do you think you were diagnosed so late?

**Partner:** What about the missed deadlines? Being late?  
**Her:** I was just called lazy. Or not trying hard enough.  
**Partner:** But the grades counted… and the struggle didn’t?  
**Her:** Exactly. A lot of that “success” was last-minute panic and adrenaline — not proof that everything was fine.  
**Close (avatar or VO):** For many women, ADHD hid behind achievement. Late recognition is common. Educational only — not a diagnosis. If this feels familiar, a licensed clinician can help you sort next steps.

### Script — Hook B (talking head)

Good grades don’t rule out ADHD in women — they often hide it.  
Teachers saw the A. They didn’t see the all-nighters, the forgotten deadlines, or the shame after every “lazy” comment.  
Many women learn to sprint on panic. From the outside it looks like potential. From the inside it feels like constant recovery.  
If school looked fine but adulthood feels like you’re failing at easy things — that gap is worth a real clinical conversation.  
Educational content only — not medical advice.

### Script — Hook A

ADHD doesn’t always look like a hyper little boy.  
In many women, it looks like inattention, disorganization, emotional intensity, and a mind that won’t quiet down — while the outside stays composed.  
Girls often mask. They people-please. They overwork. They’re called anxious or “too sensitive” long before anyone asks about ADHD.  
That’s one reason **ADHD symptoms in women** — especially **inattentive ADHD in women** — get recognized later.  
Late recognition isn’t a trend. It’s a presentation that didn’t match the stereotype.  
Educational only — not medical advice. If mood labels never quite fit, ask a clinician about lifelong attention and executive patterns.

## V4 — When hormones remove the buffer

| Field | Value |
|-------|--------|
| Intent | Education + Recognition |
| Family | E |
| Primary KW | `ADHD in women` |
| Secondary KW | `late diagnosis ADHD women` |
| On-screen support | `ADHD perimenopause` / postpartum (sparingly) |
| Length | 40–50s |
| Ship | Fourth — clinical review before publish |

### Hook archetypes

**A — Myth-busting / Informational**  
“You’re not ‘suddenly getting worse’ in your 30s or 40s.”

**B — Pattern interrupt / Relatable**  
“Motherhood or perimenopause didn’t create your ADHD — it removed the buffer that hid it.”

### Visual strategy

| Option | Format | Notes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| **Primary** | Text-on-screen + lifestyle B-roll | Getting-ready / clinic prep aesthetic (high IG performer); calm VO |
| Alt | Talking head + simple diagram | Estrogen ↔ attention systems — plain language, no DIY dosing |
| Alt | Podcast clip style | 4 practical helps (track · reduce load · sleep first · get assessed) |

### Script — Hook A (compliance-tight)

You’re not “suddenly getting worse” in your 30s or 40s.  
Many women with undiagnosed or under-recognized ADHD notice symptoms intensify when hormones shift — postpartum, cycle changes, or perimenopause.  
Estrogen interacts with brain systems involved in attention. When that buffer changes, masking gets harder: fog, overwhelm, emotional volatility, less capacity to push through.  
That doesn’t mean hormones *cause* ADHD. It means biology can reveal a pattern that was already there.  
What helps practically: track what you notice, reduce cognitive load, protect sleep, and get properly assessed — don’t sit in self-blame.  
Educational only — not medical advice. Individual care belongs with a licensed clinician.

### Caption (IG)

**ADHD in women** can feel louder after pregnancy or in perimenopause — not because you failed, but because the old buffer changed.  
**Late diagnosis ADHD women** often happens when coping strategies stop working.  

Educational only. Not a diagnosis. Discuss hormones + attention with a clinician who can see the whole picture.  
https://siya.health/blog/adhd-hormones-women

### Script — Hook B (spoken; or convert to text cards)

These aren’t personality flaws. They’re **female ADHD symptoms** — and **ADHD symptoms in women** — that people miss.  
One: intense fear of disappointing people.  
Two: looking calm while your mind runs a full checklist nobody sees.  
Three: good grades… shaky recall of what you actually learned.  
Four: so many responsibilities you freeze at the first step.  
Five: replaying a conversation for hours after everyone else moved on.  
If several feel true, that doesn’t diagnose you. It means a careful clinical history may be worth it.  
Educational only — not medical advice.

It’s okay if scrolling is what your brain does when overwhelm makes action impossible.  
These can be coping patterns — not proof you’re rude, lazy, or “too much.”  
If this has been lifelong, a clinician can help you look deeper. Educational only.
