## Preparation

### Topic decision

Research the overlap between adult ADHD care and weight-management routines, with emphasis on systems that reduce planning burden and on coordinated clinician conversations. Do not frame ADHD as a single cause of obesity or ADHD medication as a weight-loss treatment.

### 1. Why are we researching this now?

- The current canonical answer is thin: its “Detailed answer” is only a medication-stacking warning, and its evidence section names broad source categories without citations.
- A closely related carousel, `ADHD-2026-08-25-weight-feel-harder`, has already shipped. New work must avoid duplicating its hunger-cue, hyperfocus, catch-up-eating, and prep-wall sequence.
- Current social content is using a more specific, relatable angle: plans built for a “fantasy self” versus defaults that work on low-capacity days.
- Medication/appetite and GLP-1/ADHD claims are attracting 2026 attention, but the evidence does not support treating GLP-1 medicines as ADHD therapy or ADHD stimulants as obesity treatment.

### 2. Who is the target audience?

**Primary:** Adults approximately 25–50, all genders, who have diagnosed or suspected ADHD and are trying to manage weight or eating routines.

**Priority concerns/pain points:**

- Repeatedly starting new plans but not sustaining them
- Decision fatigue around meals
- Forgetting to eat, then making rushed food choices
- Shame or “I lack discipline” self-talk
- Appetite changes during ADHD treatment
- Uncertainty about discussing ADHD and weight goals with one or multiple clinicians

**Secondary:** Partners/caregivers and clinicians looking for patient-language hooks without causation claims.

### 3. What business goal does this support?

**Primary goal:** Awareness → consideration.

- Awareness: replace a character-flaw frame with a system-design frame.
- Consideration: establish Siya as able to discuss attention, appetite, nutrition patterns, and metabolic goals in a coordinated clinical context.
- Action is intentionally soft: read the current guide or bring both goals into a clinician conversation. No diagnosis-from-social or medication recommendation.

### 4. What content format should be created?

**Recommended now:** Five-slide lean A-03 carousel using a distinct “fewer decisions / real-life defaults” angle.

**Queue:**

- Short recognition reel using the same low-claim language, only after carousel approval
- Expansion outline for the thin canonical answer page, in a separate site-content task
- Clinician-reviewed FAQ on appetite/weight monitoring during ADHD treatment

**Do not create now:** medication-combination comparison, “GLP-1 for ADHD,” stimulant weight-loss explainer, before/after creative, or a second “ADHD makes weight harder” carousel.

# Step 3 — Clinical Research Documentation

## Search method

PubMed was searched directly with:

1. `(ADHD[Title/Abstract]) AND (obesity[Title/Abstract] OR weight[Title/Abstract]) AND (systematic review[Publication Type] OR meta-analysis[Publication Type]) AND (2024:2026[pdat])`
2. `(ADHD[Title/Abstract]) AND (eating behavior[Title/Abstract] OR binge eating[Title/Abstract]) AND (systematic review[Publication Type] OR meta-analysis[Publication Type]) AND (2024:2026[pdat])`
3. A widened 2021–2026 search after the recent results did not yield a direct systematic review of practical, ADHD-adapted adult weight-management strategies.

**Finding:** Recent evidence supports the ADHD–obesity association and selected medication/appetite questions, but there is limited recent systematic-review evidence for specific everyday interventions such as default meals or visible backup foods. Those tactics should be framed as practical supports, not “research-proven” outcomes.

## Relevant screened records

### 1. Kang et al. (2024)

- **Title:** Comorbid health conditions in people with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorders: An umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses
- **Journal:** Asian Journal of Psychiatry, 99, 104135
- **PMID:** [39003821](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39003821/)
- **DOI:** [10.1016/j.ajp.2024.104135](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajp.2024.104135)
- **Design:** Umbrella review; 22 meta-analyses, 544 primary studies, more than 234 million participants
- **Relevant finding:** High-certainty evidence for an association between ADHD and obesity; lower-certainty evidence for eating disorders and type 2 diabetes.
- **Limitation for content:** Association does not establish a single causal pathway or justify diagnosing either condition from the other.
- **Marketing claim risk:** **HIGH** if used on-frame or as “research shows.”

### 2. Caci, Chierici & Iannelli (2024)

- **Title:** Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in adolescent and adult candidates for metabolic and bariatric surgery: A systematic review and meta-analysis
- **Journal:** Obesity Reviews, 25(10), e13802
- **PMID:** [39040012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39040012/)
- **DOI:** [10.1111/obr.13802](https://doi.org/10.1111/obr.13802)
- **Design:** 14 adult studies (24,455 adults) and three adolescent studies
- **Relevant finding:** ADHD appeared more frequently in metabolic/bariatric surgery candidates than in the general population.
- **Limitations:** Study quality was moderate to poor; treatment-seeking bariatric candidates are not representative of all adults with ADHD or all adults pursuing weight loss.
- **Marketing claim risk:** **HIGH**; do not use prevalence numbers in this pack.

### 3. Vedrenne-Gutiérrez et al. (2024)

- **Title:** Methylphenidate can help reduce weight, appetite, and food intake—a narrative review of adults’ anthropometric changes and feeding behaviors
- **Journal:** Frontiers in Nutrition, 11, 1497772
- **PMID:** [39677498](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39677498/)
- **DOI:** [10.3389/fnut.2024.1497772](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1497772)
- **Design:** Review of 39 studies, including 14 randomized trials and three observational studies
- **Relevant finding:** Appetite suppression and modest weight effects were reported.
- **Limitations:** The review itself calls for more evidence on safety, long-term efficacy, and sex-specific effects. It does not establish methylphenidate as obesity treatment.
- **Marketing claim risk:** **HIGH** and §7.3 Legal trigger if promoted as efficacy or treatment comparison. Parked.

### 4. Vickers et al. (2024)

- **Title:** Stimulant medications in the management of bulimia nervosa and anorexia nervosa in patients with and without comorbid attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: A systematic review
- **Journal:** Eating Behaviors, 54, 101908
- **PMID:** [39137484](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39137484/)
- **DOI:** [10.1016/j.eatbeh.2024.101908](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eatbeh.2024.101908)
- **Design:** 13 articles; evidence largely consisted of case reports/series
- **Relevant finding:** The authors found insufficient evidence to support stimulants for treating bulimia nervosa or anorexia nervosa; adverse effects included appetite/weight and cardiovascular effects.
- **Content implication:** Do not turn anecdotal appetite or eating changes into a treatment recommendation.
- **Marketing claim risk:** **HIGH** if used as a treatment claim. Parked.

## Clinical conclusion

The clinically defensible public message is:

- ADHD and obesity can be associated, but that does not mean ADHD alone causes obesity.
- ADHD treatment may affect appetite or weight for some people; monitoring belongs with the prescriber.
- Specific medication combinations, efficacy, safety, and weight outcomes require individualized clinical review.
- The proposed carousel should stay on low-claim system design and a medium-risk coordination message.
