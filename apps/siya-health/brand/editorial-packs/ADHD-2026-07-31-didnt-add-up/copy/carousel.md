# Carousel — "Something still doesn't add up" (recognition stories)

**Insight:** The most shareable ADHD content isn't education — it's recognition. The
common thread across these composite experiences is not ADHD; it's *"I knew something
wasn't adding up."* People don't feel taught. They feel seen.

**Practical change:** If symptoms persist despite treatment — or a clinical picture has
never quite made sense — bring the full history to a structured, whole-person evaluation
rather than stopping at the first label that partly fits.

**Tone locks:** calm · reassuring · physician-led · no fear language · we never say
"you probably have ADHD" — we say "these experiences deserve a thoughtful evaluation" ·
second opinions are about **accuracy in either direction**, never diagnosis-shopping.

**Compliance locks (non-negotiable):**

1. Stories are **composites** of experiences people commonly describe publicly — never
   presented as real patient stories or quoted from identifiable individuals. The hook
   slide says so on-frame.
2. Slide 4 must include the "second opinion can also confirm it is *not* ADHD" sentence.
   Without it the arc reads as diagnosis-shopping (the Cerebral/Done narrative).
3. No prevalence numbers on-frame. Comorbidity stated as "often occur together" only.

**Sibling pack:** `ADHD-2026-07-31-conditions-alongside` covers the comorbidity theme in
teaching voice. This pack is the same territory in recognition voice — do not publish
both in the same week.

## Slide 2 — "I was told it was depression." (1 of 3)

**Headline:** "I was told it was depression."
**Recognition:** Therapy helped. Medication helped. But something still didn't make
sense.
**Explanation:** ADHD and depression often occur together. Treating depression matters —
and if ADHD is also present, focus, motivation, and follow-through can stay hard even as
mood improves. [1][2]
**Takeaway:** Progress in one area doesn't rule out a second explanation.

---

## Slide 3 — "My brain has always felt like a tree." (2 of 3)

**Headline:** "My brain has always felt like a tree."
**Recognition:** One thought becomes another… then another… until something interrupts
me.
**Explanation:** Many people with ADHD describe a mind that constantly branches. Thinking
this way doesn't mean you have ADHD — but it's an experience many recognize.
**Takeaway:** Recognition isn't diagnosis. It's a reason to ask better questions.

---

## Slide 4 — "I was told it wasn't ADHD." (3 of 3)

**Headline:** "I was told it wasn't ADHD."
**Recognition:** At 65 — disorganized, procrastinating, interrupting, anxious. One
clinician said no. The picture stayed confusing.
**Explanation:** ADHD assessment is genuinely complex — symptoms overlap with anxiety,
depression, and trauma. When the picture stays unclear, a second thorough evaluation can
help — including by confirming it is *not* ADHD. [2][3]
**Takeaway:** A second opinion is about accuracy — in either direction.

---

## Slide 5 — Different conditions can look alike

**Headline:** Different conditions can look alike.
**Explanation:** Depression and ADHD can both stall motivation. Anxiety and ADHD can both
crowd the mind. Trauma and ADHD can both scatter concentration. [2][3]
**Takeaway:** Sometimes it's one. Sometimes the other. Sometimes both.

---

## Slide 6 — The goal isn't to collect diagnoses

**Headline:** The goal isn't to collect diagnoses.
**Explanation:** It's to understand what's actually causing your symptoms. The right care
depends on the full picture — not the first label that partly fits.
**Takeaway:** Understanding beats labeling.
