# ENTRY A — ADHD interventions umbrella review (BMJ)

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Capture ID** | `PMED-2026-08-06-adhd-interventions-umbrella` |
| **Track** | PubMed/academic |
| **Citation** | Gosling CJ, et al. Benefits and harms of ADHD interventions: umbrella review and platform for shared decision making. *BMJ*. 2025;391:e085875. |
| **URL** | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12651917/ |
| **PMCID / PMID** | PMC12651917 / 41097970 *(verify on PMC landing)* |
| **Design** | Umbrella review of meta-analyses of RCTs + living evidence platform |
| **Scope** | Drug and non-drug ADHD interventions across lifespan vs passive control |
| **Scale note** | 115 eligible meta-analytic reports → **221** re-estimated PICO combinations from **47** reports; **31** interventions · **24** outcomes |
| **Evidence tool** | Algorithmic GRADE (high / moderate / low / very low); AMSTAR-2 on included MAs |
| **Living resource** | https://ebiadhd-database.org/ |
| **Clinical review required** | **Yes — Dr. Swati Pandey** before any public claim under her name |
| **Publish status** | Banked · not approved |

### 1. Core finding (plain language)

A large evidence map of ADHD treatments shows that several **medications** (including methylphenidate and, depending on age group, amphetamines, atomoxetine, alpha-2 agonists, and viloxazine) have **moderate-to-high certainty** short-term benefit for ADHD symptoms in many analyses — especially in children and adolescents. In adults, methylphenidate, atomoxetine, cognitive behavioural therapy, and (when limited to higher-quality trials) amphetamines also show at least moderate-certainty short-term benefit. **Long-term high-certainty evidence is largely missing**, and some popular non-drug approaches look large in effect size but sit on **low-certainty** evidence — so “what works” and “how sure we are” must be stated together.

### 2. Key stats / evidence-strength (do not overstate)

| Claim surface | Strength | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Methylphenidate short-term symptom benefit (children/adolescents), consistent across raters | **Moderate–high** certainty; SMD often **>0.75** (e.g. CI spans ~0.56–1.03 in reported clinician/parent/teacher analyses) | Short-term window (~12 weeks). Side effects / tolerability still matter. |
| Alpha-2 agonists, amphetamines, atomoxetine, viloxazine (pediatric short-term) | **Moderate–high** for several symptom outcomes | Effect sizes and rater agreement vary (amphetamines: clinician ratings stronger than parent/teacher in some analyses). |
| Adults: atomoxetine, CBT, methylphenidate (± amphetamines in high-quality-trial sensitivity) | **≥ Moderate** certainty for short-term symptoms | Adult amphetamine certainty depends on quality filters. |
| Adult drug tolerability (MPH, amphetamines, atomoxetine) | **High** certainty of **worse** tolerability vs placebo (e.g. MPH RR ~0.50 for tolerability outcome as reported) | “Works” ≠ “everyone stays on it.” |
| Acupuncture / CBT (youth), mindfulness (adults) — large-looking effects | **Low / very low** certainty despite large SMDs | Easy to overclaim in social content — **do not** present as proven equals to stimulants. |
| Medium / long-term (26–52 weeks) | **No high or moderate certainty** for any intervention in this map | Living evidence gap — say “we don’t yet have strong long-term certainty,” not “treatments don’t work long-term.” |

# ENTRY B — ADHD medication in pregnancy (systematic review)

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Capture ID** | `PMED-2026-08-06-adhd-meds-pregnancy-sr` |
| **Track** | PubMed/academic |
| **Citation** | Taylor S, et al. Maternal and offspring outcomes associated with prescribed ADHD medication in pregnancy: a systematic review. *Arch Womens Ment Health*. 2025;28(6):1425–1446. |
| **URL** | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12702805/ |
| **PMCID / PMID** | PMC12702805 / 41083615 |
| **Design** | Systematic review (narrative synthesis; **no** meta-analysis) of cohort studies, last 5 years (Jul 2019–Jul 2024) |
| **Included studies** | **12** cohort studies; all rated high quality (NOS ≥ 7) |
| **Aggregate N** | ~**7.56 million** participants across studies (individual studies 149 → 4.2M+) |
| **Exposure** | Prescribed ADHD meds (stimulants, atomoxetine, guanfacine, clonidine, modafinil, etc.) |
| **Clinical review required** | **Yes — Dr. Swati Pandey** (perinatal + ADHD; high-stakes claims) |
| **Publish status** | Banked · not approved |

### 1. Core finding (plain language)

Recent studies (last five years) mostly do **not** show a clear, large harm signal from continuing prescribed ADHD medication in pregnancy for many mother and baby outcomes — and some work suggests **stopping** may carry risks (for example one study linked stopping dexamfetamine with higher odds of threatened abortion). A few signals still need caution: **modafinil** was linked with higher odds of congenital malformations; some older-style associations (certain birth defects, pre-eclampsia) appear in individual studies but often shrink after better adjustment for ADHD itself. The review’s practical message is **shared decision-making**, not a blanket “always continue” or “always stop.”

### 2. Key stats / evidence-strength (do not overstate)

| Claim surface | Strength | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Overall: many studies find **no significant negative** association for continued ADHD meds | **Narrative** synthesis of **12** high-NOS cohorts — **not** a pooled meta-analysis | Heterogeneous meds, timing, outcomes; prescription ≠ confirmed ingestion in most registries. |
| Bang Madsen et al.: continuers vs discontinuers, neurodevelopmental outcomes | HR ~**0.97** (95% CI 0.81–1.17) — no increased risk signal in that analysis | One study among many; still observational. |
| Russell et al.: stopping dexamfetamine ↔ threatened abortion | OR **2.28** (95% CI 1.00–5.15); n≈**1,688** | Authors of the SR note **small sample** and non-replication in larger studies → **possible reporting bias** — **do not** lead content with this OR. |
| Damkier: first-trimester **modafinil** ↔ major malformations | OR **~2.7–3.4** vs no med / methylphenidate | **Clearest drug-specific red flag** in this SR — “consider stopping before pregnancy” is the paper’s line; Siya must not soft-pedal or over-generalize to all ADHD meds. |
| Anderson et al.: early pregnancy ADHD meds ↔ gastroschisis / omphalocele / limb deficiency | Crude ORs ~**2.9–4.0** | Crude / limited adjustment in places; **rare outcomes**; easy to terrify — needs MD wording. |
| Camacho et al.: raw adverse perinatal rates higher; most attenuate after adjustment | Pre-eclampsia signal **inconclusive** after weighting | Residual confounding by indication remains central. |
| Dose / duration | **Not studied** in included papers | Cannot claim trimester-specific dosing safety from this SR. |

**Authors’ conclusion (paraphrase for bank only):** Women on **modafinil** should consider stopping before pregnancy; for other ADHD meds, clinicians should discuss risks, benefits, and **uncertainties**, keeping in mind that for someone who benefits from medication, **benefits of continuing may outweigh risks** — this is a **clinical counseling frame**, not Siya public medical advice until Dr. Swati signs off.

**Not for post until clinical review.**

### 4. Safety / “talk to your clinician” caveats (required before publish)

- **Perinatal content is high-risk.** Every piece needs explicit: *This is education, not personal medical advice. Decisions about ADHD medication in pregnancy belong with your prescribing clinician and obstetric team.*  
- Never tell patients to continue or discontinue a specific regimen (except reflecting **modafinil** caution **as literature**, still with clinician language).  
- Do not present “benefits outweigh risks” as universal — the paper frames it for people for whom medication is effective.  
- Do not use fear imagery around birth defects; rare absolute risks need careful absolute-risk context if numbers are used at all (MD decides).  
- Siya serves adults in CA/TX/PA/FL — pregnancy content must not imply Siya manages obstetric care end-to-end.  
- Controlled-substance / prescribing laws — no “we’ll keep you on X through pregnancy” promises.
