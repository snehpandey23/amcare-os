## Post (publish this)

During internship, I was convinced I'd become a pulmonologist and intensivist.

I loved the ICU.

ACLS. CPR. Intubations.

The moments where everyone in the room knows exactly what needs to happen, and for a few minutes nothing else exists.

It felt like *this* was medicine.

Then I spent more time there.

The adrenaline became familiar.

What stayed with me weren't the procedures.

It was the conversations afterwards.

Walking into a room where a family had just watched someone's life change forever.

Explaining what we knew.

Explaining what we didn't.

Sitting with decisions nobody ever wants to make.

That's when something shifted.

I realized I didn't want to spend my career getting really good at helping people after everything had already gone wrong.

I wanted to spend it helping fewer people get there in the first place.

That's what eventually drew me to primary care.

People often describe primary care as "less exciting."

Maybe.

There aren't many intubations in clinic.

No code blues.

No dramatic saves.

Just hundreds of small decisions made months or years before anyone ever needs an ICU bed.

The longer I practice, the more I think we've got the story backwards.

Saving a life in the ICU is extraordinary.

Helping someone never need the ICU at all might be one of the greatest successes in medicine.

I'm incredibly grateful for the time I spent in critical care.

It didn't convince me to stay.

It taught me where I could make the biggest difference.
