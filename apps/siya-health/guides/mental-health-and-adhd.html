Mental health and ADHD

You've reread the same email three times and still can't start the reply. The tabs stay open. The deadline moves. On paper you're "fine" — work gets done somehow — but the trail of unfinished things keeps growing. If that's familiar, you're far from alone. Trouble focusing in adult life can mean a lot of different things — ADHD that was always there, burnout that arrived with the job, anxiety that steals attention, fog from poor sleep or low iron — sometimes more than one at once. This page walks through how adult ADHD is actually defined, how a quick screen differs from a real evaluation, and how clinicians sort focus problems from the lookalikes.

Educational only: This page is for general education—not personal medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. See a licensed clinician for your situation.

Clinician-informed

Educational content informed by clinical practice patterns—not personal medical advice.

How it shows up in an adult work life

Adult ADHD — attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder — is a DSM-5-TR diagnosis (the current psychiatric manual), not a busy week. For people 17 and older it requires all of the following: 1

  • A persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity.
  • At least five symptoms in one or both of those groups.
  • At least six months of that pattern, with clear impairment.
  • At least two settings — for example work and home — not only one hard season at the office.
  • Several symptoms before age 12, even when the person first names the pattern as an adult.
  • Not better explained by another mental disorder.

In adults the inattentive pattern is often what gets noticed:

  • Losing the thread of tasks or not finishing
  • Difficulty organizing
  • Avoiding work that takes sustained mental effort
  • Losing things, easy distraction, forgetting daily activities

Hyperactivity in adults may show up as restlessness or fidgeting rather than childhood running and climbing. 1 None of these, as a single week at work, meets the diagnosis.

  • “High-functioning” is not a DSM specifier. Adults can meet criteria and still perform well in some roles while impaired in others — the diagnosis requires impairment, not global failure. 1
  • Sex pattern. In the DSM-5-TR, females are more likely than males to present primarily with inattentive features. 1
  • Late recognition. Naming the pattern for the first time as an adult does not remove the childhood-onset requirement. The history still has to show the pattern was there earlier, not only that adult life got harder. 1

Screening, and how that differs from an evaluation

The Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS) was developed with the World Health Organization as a screening questionnaire. A research form aligned to DSM-5 is also a screen. A score suggests whether a full clinical assessment is worth doing. It does not diagnose. 23

What a screen cannot tell you:

  • Whether you meet diagnostic criteria
  • Whether anxiety, depression, sleep problems, or medical illness better explain the symptoms
  • Which treatment, if any, is appropriate

An evaluation is a clinical interview. Typical pieces include: 14

  • The diagnostic criteria applied to your history
  • Childhood history and impairment in more than one part of life
  • A look for other explanations — mood, anxiety, substances, sleep, and medical illness

A questionnaire finished in a few minutes is the screen. It is not that interview. A visit that starts from a screen is an evaluation, not a promise of a prescription. Medication can be one part of care after evaluation; which medicine, if any, is decided in the visit. This page does not name medicines or describe a dose.

Other readings: anxiety, mood, burnout, fog, and thyroid

More than one of these can be true at once. Naming them does not diagnose you. The DSM requires that ADHD symptoms not be better explained by another mental disorder. 1

  • Anxiety. Anxiety disorders center on excessive fear or worry. An evaluation sorts attention stolen by feared situations from a long attention-and-organization pattern across home and work that began earlier in life. Both can be present. Missed deadlines and shame can also bring anxiety — assess both rather than the first label. 1
  • Depression and other mood disorders. Low mood, loss of interest, and slowed thinking can look like poor focus. The DSM treats mood disorders as explanations that must be weighed before ADHD is assigned. Both can still be present; the history and current episode timing are how they are sorted. 1
  • Burnout. In the WHO classification, burnout is an occupational phenomenon from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed — not classified as a medical condition. 5 It can ease when the work situation changes. It does not replace a developmental history. If the pattern existed long before the job and shows up outside work, burnout may not be enough. If it started with the job and eases with rest, ADHD may not be enough.
  • Fog. “Brain fog” here means slowed thinking, blanking on words, or mental fatigue. It is a complaint, not a diagnosis. Anxiety, short sleep, and low iron can each contribute. Those workups are not repeated here.
  • Thyroid. Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) can include forgetfulness among other nonspecific symptoms and needs blood testing to confirm — not a focus-story alone. 6
  • Rejection sensitivity. Intense pain after perceived criticism is a description some people with ADHD use. It is not a DSM diagnosis.

Follow-through at work

  • Inside the DSM list. Difficulty finishing, organizing, and starting tasks that take sustained effort is already part of the inattention criteria. 1
  • Everyday language. “Executive dysfunction” (trouble planning, starting, and finishing) and “time blindness” (losing track of time) are ways people describe that cluster. They are not separate DSM diagnoses.
  • Accommodations. A clinician can describe functional limits when the evaluation supports them. Whether an employer provides a change at work is an employment-law and policy question, not a medical order from this page. This page does not give legal advice.

What to bring to a visit

A short set of notes beats a vague “I can’t focus” in the exam room. Bring what you have:

  • Examples: a few recent weeks of missed starts, unfinished tasks, lost items, or deadline slips — and where they happened (work, home, both).
  • Childhood clues: school comments, report cards, or family memories about attention, restlessness, or unfinished work before age 12.
  • Other symptoms: worry, panic, low mood, sleep quality, snoring, menstrual or menopause-transition changes, and any sense of mental fog or forgetfulness.
  • Medication and substance list: prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, supplements, caffeine, alcohol, and other substances — with start dates when you know them.
  • Prior screens or scores: any ASRS or similar questionnaire you already completed, and prior mental-health or medical diagnoses.

What to discuss with your doctor

  • How long focus and follow-through have been hard, and whether that pattern was already there in childhood.
  • Whether anxiety, low mood, or a job change lined up with the worst stretch.
  • Sleep quantity and quality, including unrefreshing nights.
  • Whether fog, forgetfulness, cycle changes, or menopause-transition symptoms are part of the picture.
  • What a screening score means — and what it cannot decide.
  • Whether an evaluation (not only a questionnaire) is the next step.
  • Treatment options in general terms after evaluation — without assuming a medicine will be prescribed.
  • Urgent concerns: thoughts of self-harm, inability to care for yourself, or sudden confusion that is new and severe.
