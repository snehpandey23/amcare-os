Testosterone

If you have been told your testosterone is “low,” or you have been wondering whether fatigue, low desire, or weaker morning erections mean you need testosterone therapy, this section explains how clinicians decide—and why a symptom list alone is not enough.

A lot of people meet this topic through ads promising vigor or muscle. The clinical question is narrower: do you have symptoms consistent with testosterone deficiency and blood levels that are clearly, repeatedly low—after other causes are considered? 1

Free testosterone vs total testosterone (plain language)

  • Total testosterone — the overall amount of testosterone measured in a blood sample (bound + unbound).
  • Free testosterone — the smaller portion that is not tightly attached to carrier proteins and is more available to tissues.

Think of total as the whole pile, and free as the portion that is loose enough to act. Both can matter; a single “normal” total result is not always the whole story. 1

Why symptoms alone are not enough

The Endocrine Society recommends diagnosing hypogonadism (clinically meaningful testosterone deficiency) only when both are true: 1

  • Symptoms and signs consistent with low testosterone are present, and
  • Blood testosterone is unequivocally and consistently low

Why that bar? Tiredness, low mood, and poor concentration show up in sleep problems, depression, stress, thyroid disease, and many other conditions. Treating a lab number—or a vague symptom—without that combination risks missing the real driver and starting therapy that is not indicated. 1

How the blood test is usually done (what you can actually do)

  • Morning draw (around 8 a.m.) — Testosterone is typically higher in the morning in men; guidelines use a morning total testosterone as the starting test so the result is comparable to the evidence base. 1
  • Fasting — Food and some metabolic states can shift levels; fasting morning testing is the recommended starting approach. 1
  • Repeat on a separate morning — One low value can be a blip. Diagnosis needs a clear, repeated pattern, not a single draw. 1
  • Accurate assay — An assay is the specific lab method used to measure the hormone. Guidelines call for an accurate assay so the number is trustworthy enough to base a diagnosis on. 1

When total testosterone is near the lower end of the reference range, or when conditions change binding proteins, the guideline recommends measuring free testosterone by equilibrium dialysis or calculating it with an accurate formula—not relying on total alone. 1

Optional: how testosterone travels in the blood (bound vs free)

Most testosterone in blood is attached (“bound”) to proteins. The main tight binder is sex hormone–binding globulin (SHBG). Free testosterone is the fraction that is not tightly bound. When SHBG is high or low, total testosterone can look different from free testosterone—which is why clinicians sometimes order both. Skip this box if you only needed the testing steps above. 1

Symptoms that prompt an evaluation—and why they are not diagnostic alone

Symptoms and signs that often lead a clinician to check testosterone include reduced sexual desire, fewer spontaneous erections, fatigue, depressed mood, loss of body hair, and reduced muscle mass, among others listed in the Endocrine Society guideline. 1

These symptoms are non-specific. They overlap with many other causes. Fatigue or concentration trouble by themselves do not establish low testosterone. 1

In a large European study of middle-aged and older men, the symptom pattern that tracked most clearly with documented late-onset hypogonadism (low testosterone plus the clinical picture) was a cluster of sexual symptoms—reduced morning erections, reduced sexual thoughts, and erectile dysfunction—rather than general complaints like fatigue or mood changes alone. Other symptoms were less specifically tied to low testosterone. 7

Low testosterone and a longstanding attention disorder can coexist. Labs plus a developmental history help sort them; having one does not rule out the other. Related reading: exhaustion and mental health and ADHD.

When therapy may be considered

Guideline-level therapy discussion is for men with symptomatic testosterone deficiency after benefits, risks, and monitoring are reviewed together. Therapy is not recommended when fertility is planned in the near term, and it is avoided in a list of situations that includes breast or prostate cancer, a concerning prostate exam or PSA, elevated hematocrit, untreated severe obstructive sleep apnea, severe urinary symptoms, uncontrolled heart failure, recent heart attack or stroke, and thrombophilia. 1

Routine testosterone for every man 65+ who merely has a low lab value is not recommended. In that age group, therapy is only a suggestion when suggestive symptoms and consistently low morning levels are both present, after an explicit risk–benefit talk. 1 This page does not say you are a candidate.

What a testosterone visit actually involves

A visit is an evaluation, not a promise of a prescription. It usually includes a general health check for illness, eating disorders, heavy exercise, sleep disorders, and medicines or substances that can suppress testosterone (for example opioids or high-dose glucocorticoids). Obesity is one reversible contributor the guideline discusses. 1 The sleep guide and exhaustion guide often belong in the same conversation.

Honest limits for this section: education about how diagnosis is made—not a personal diagnosis, not dosing advice, and not a claim that therapy will be prescribed.

Perimenopause and menopause (mood, focus, and hormone therapy)

If you are in your 40s or 50s, your cycles are changing, and you are trying to sort whether brain fog, sleep breaks, or hot flashes are “just aging,” hormones, ADHD, or something else—this section is for you.

Perimenopause vs menopause—said plainly

  • Perimenopause — the transition years before menopause, when cycles often become irregular and symptoms like hot flashes, night sweats, sleep disruption, or mood change can appear.
  • Menopause — the point marked after you have gone 12 months in a row without a period (when that pattern is not explained by another cause such as pregnancy, surgery, or certain medicines). 3

Perimenopause is the path. Menopause is the landmark after a full year without bleeding.

Mood, focus, and what often travels with the transition

Many midlife women notice changes in memory, attention, or concentration during the menopause transition. An International Menopause Society white paper says those cognitive complaints should not be confused with dementia, that dementia in midlife is rare, and that most women who go through menopause will not develop dementia. 2

Depression, sleep problems, and hot flashes/night sweats in perimenopause are associated with cognitive complaints. 2 That is a reason to ask about sleep and mood—see the sleep guide and mental health and ADHD guide—not a shortcut to a hormone prescription.

Adult ADHD remains a long-pattern diagnosis (several symptoms before age 12, impairment in more than one setting, not better explained by another condition). 4 A change around a cycle or around menopause does not create that history, and it does not erase it. Females are more likely than males to present mainly with inattentive features, which is why the history has to be taken carefully. 4 This page does not claim that month-to-month hormone swings reliably change ADHD scores.

Hormone therapy: two different claims (keep them separate)

  1. Hot flashes and night sweats: Hormone therapy can be an effective treatment for bothersome vasomotor symptoms, with its own individualized risk discussion. This page is not a prescribing guide for that decision. 3
  2. Cognition and dementia prevention: Hormone therapy is not recommended to treat cognitive complaints or to prevent dementia. 3

Iron deficiency and a sleep disorder can look like the same midlife story—see exhaustion and sleep. A headache that tracks the cycle, or a new headache in these years, belongs in a visit conversation on the headache page.

What a visit involves here: bleeding pattern, nights, mood, medicines, and which symptom is actually limiting work. A hormone discussion, when it happens, follows that history. It is an evaluation—not a promise of a prescription.

Honest limits for this section: definitions, evaluation framing, and what major position statements say about hormone therapy indications—not personal menopause staging and not a treatment plan.

Sexual health

What a proper erectile-dysfunction visit includes

Erectile dysfunction is a reason for a medical, sexual, and psychosocial history—and for counseling that it can be a risk marker for cardiovascular disease and other conditions that may need their own evaluation. 5 Shared risk factors include age, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, abnormal lipids, depression, obesity, and inactivity. 5

A licensed clinician reviews your history and current medications before anything is prescribed. That is different from setups that issue a prescription without a real health review. The visit is that evaluation. It is not a promise of a prescription.

What sildenafil is, in that visit

Men with erectile dysfunction should be informed about an FDA-approved oral PDE5 inhibitor as a treatment option, including benefits and risks, unless it is contraindicated. 5 Sildenafil is in that class. It must not be combined with nitrate medicines. Current labeling contraindicates sildenafil with organic nitrates or nitrites because it can deepen the blood-pressure drop from nitrates; after a dose, it is unknown when a nitrate can be given safely. 6 Someone who needs emergency care for chest pain should say a PDE5 inhibitor was taken. 5

Psychological contributors belong in the history. 5 This page does not give a dose or promise a prescription.
