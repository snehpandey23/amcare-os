Health guide

Exhausted, with no explanation

If you are a busy professional who feels tired, flat, or underperforming despite a full calendar and what looks like enough sleep, you are not alone—and tiredness by itself still does not name the cause. This page is for that in-between moment: when you know something is off, but you do not yet know which branch comes next:

  • Sleep
  • Labs
  • Mood
  • Hormones
  • Attention
  • Something else entirely

Educational only: This page is for general education—not personal medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. See a licensed clinician for your situation.

Clinician-informed

Educational content informed by clinical practice patterns—not personal medical advice.

What this pattern is

Persistent exhaustion with a full schedule is a common reason adults seek care. The complaint is real. The label is not automatic. The same story can sit over more than one contributor at once, and a visit is an evaluation—not a promise of a prescription.

What this page covers—and what it does not

Covers:

  • How to recognize the pattern
  • How fatigue is measured in research
  • How to track your own energy so a visit has something concrete to work with

  • What to raise with a clinician

Does not cover:

  • A personal diagnosis
  • Dosing
  • A promise that labs or a medicine will be ordered
  • A full workup for every possible cause listed below

How fatigue is measured (not guessed)

In research and many specialty clinics, “I’m exhausted” is turned into scores with validated questionnaires—not because a quiz online can diagnose you, but because clinicians and studies need a repeatable way to describe severity and change over time. Instruments you may hear named include:

  • Chalder Fatigue Scale (Fatigue Questionnaire). A short self-rating scale developed to measure fatigue severity in research settings. 1
  • Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS). A nine-item scale originally studied in neurologic and autoimmune disease that rates how much fatigue interferes with daily living. 2
  • Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI). A questionnaire that separates facets such as general fatigue, physical fatigue, mental fatigue, reduced motivation, and reduced activity. 3

You do not need to score yourself at home. Knowing these tools exist helps you understand what a careful evaluation is aiming at: a clear description of how bad it is, how long it has lasted, and how it is changing—not a single blood number that “proves” you are fine.

Track your pattern before the visit

Two weeks of notes beat a vague “I’m always tired” in the exam room. Keep it simple—phone notes or a paper grid are enough:

  • Energy: morning / afternoon / evening score from 0–10, plus one word for the kind of tired (sleepy, heavy limbs, wired-and-tired, mentally blank).
  • Sleep — note each of:
    • Bedtime and wake time
    • Estimated hours asleep
    • Whether the night felt restorative
    • Snoring, gasping, or witnessed pauses a partner noticed
  • Schedule load: late meetings, night or rotating shifts, caregiving, travel, or days with no real break.
  • Mood and stress: low mood, anxiety spikes, or a sustained sense of overload—even if you are still “functioning.”
  • Body clues: heavy periods, cycle changes, cold intolerance, unexplained weight change, shortness of breath with stairs, new headache pattern.
  • Medications and substances: prescriptions, over-the-counter sleep aids, alcohol, caffeine timing.

Bring the log. Patterns across workdays versus days off, or energy that never recovers after a full night in bed, are clinically useful even before any test is ordered.

What primary-care research finds most often

Tiredness is one of the most frequent reasons people see a primary-care clinician. Across studies of adults who present with tiredness, serious new cancer is uncommon; depression, sleep problems, and psychosocial strain show up far more often than rare organic surprises. 45

A systematic review and meta-analysis of primary-care studies of people presenting with tiredness reported these pooled prevalences among the diagnoses those studies measured: 5

  1. Depression — about 18.5% (95% CI 16.2–21.0%). The most common diagnosis in that pooled set.
  2. Serious somatic (organic) disease — about 4.3% (95% CI 2.7–6.7%). A mixed bucket; still far less common than depression in those studies.
  3. Anaemia — about 2.8% (95% CI 1.6–4.8%). Hemoglobin below the person’s reference range; iron deficiency can also matter before anaemia appears (see below).
  4. Malignancy — about 0.6% (95% CI 0.3–1.3%). Rare as the explanation for undifferentiated tiredness in those primary-care samples.

That meta-analysis did not pool sleep disorders the same way. Separate primary-care reviews still name sleep disorders and sleep-related breathing problems, along with depression and excessive psychosocial stress, among the most common contributors to persistent fatigue. 4 A large primary-care cohort also found especially strong associations with depression, insomnia/sleep disturbance, and (in women) thyroid disease. 6

Iron deficiency and anaemia — what to understand in triage

Iron deficiency can cause fatigue, weakness, shortness of breath with activity, headache, and trouble concentrating. Those symptoms are nonspecific, and they can appear before or without anaemia. 7

  • Anaemia means hemoglobin below the range used for that person; hemoglobin alone does not prove the cause is iron. 89
  • Clinicians use a blood count plus iron studies, including ferritin; ferritin can be misleading when inflammation is present. 9
  • A normal hemoglobin does not by itself rule iron deficiency out. 7
  • Finding low iron is also a prompt to ask why iron is low, not only to treat the number. 7

Thyroid — what to understand in triage

Hypothyroidism means the thyroid is not making enough hormone. When levels are low, the body can slow. Commonly listed symptoms include:

  • Fatigue
  • Feeling cold
  • Dry skin
  • Constipation
  • Low mood
  • Forgetfulness

Not everyone has all of them, and other problems cause the same symptoms. A blood test is required to know. 10 Usual first tests are TSH and, when indicated, free T4. An isolated symptom list is not the diagnosis. 1011 Thyroid disease and iron deficiency can both sit under the same complaint; finding one does not rule the other out.

Sleep that does not restore you

Any of the following can leave you exhausted even after “enough” hours in bed:

  • Unrefreshing sleep
  • Snoring, gasping, or witnessed breathing pauses
  • A work schedule that collides with normal sleep hours

Shift-work strain (missing sleep because of the job) is not automatically the same as a diagnosable sleep disorder. Sorting the next layer is part of the history—and sometimes formal sleep testing—not something a page can finish for you:

  • Obstructive sleep apnea
  • Insomnia
  • Excessive sleepiness
  • Schedule strain without a named sleep disorder

Hormones, cycle changes, and low testosterone

Fatigue that tracks menstrual-cycle changes or the menopause transition belongs in the history, as does a pattern of low desire, reduced morning energy, and other symptoms that raise a question of low testosterone in men. Low testosterone is diagnosed from symptoms plus repeatedly low morning testosterone levels—not from fatigue alone. 12

What to discuss with your doctor

  • How long this has lasted, and whether it started with a job change, illness, schedule shift, pregnancy/postpartum period, or medication change.
  • Your two-week energy/sleep log (or the best notes you have).
  • Whether sleep feels unrefreshing, and any snoring, gasping, or witnessed pauses.
  • Mood: persistent low mood, loss of interest, anxiety that will not settle, or a sense of burnout that does not lift on days off.
  • Bleeding history (heavy periods), cycle changes, or menopause-transition symptoms if relevant.
  • Symptoms that raise a question of low iron or thyroid (breathlessness on stairs, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, unexplained weight change).
  • Whether difficulty concentrating feels lifelong across settings, or mainly showed up when sleep and energy collapsed.
  • Which medicines, supplements, alcohol, and caffeine timing are in the picture.
  • Any red flags that need urgent care rather than a routine visit: chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, confusion, or thoughts of self-harm.
